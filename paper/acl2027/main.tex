\author{Anonymous ACL submission}

\begin{abstract}
GUI agents are increasingly capable of completing everyday computer tasks, yet their success is usually judged by whether the requested action was executed. For users with accessibility needs, an apparently completed task may still leave inaccessible information, an incorrect support artifact, or an unusable interface. We introduce \bench, the first executable desktop GUI-agent benchmark that explicitly evaluates accessibility-conditioned handoff. Its 198 tasks cover 14 ISO/IEC 29138-1-grounded needs across visual, hearing, motor, and cognitive scenarios. Each task is evaluated for nominal task completion ($T$), handoff readiness ($H$), and full success ($F=T\land H$). Evaluation of four strong GUI-agent baselines reveals systematic failures in evidence transfer, persistent artifacts, assistive-state preservation, and completion verification. Across 792 runs, 25 complete the nominal task but fail handoff, while 81 establish support without completing the task correctly. Guided by these findings, we develop LocalLSTC2, a condition-driven execution method that converts the requested terminal outcome into fixed delivery conditions, executes and verifies them sequentially, carries task-critical facts across condition boundaries, and filters software APIs for the active condition. On a controlled 60-task split, it improves mean full success from 56.7 to 63.3, with task completion rising from 60.0 to 65.0 and handoff readiness from 63.3 to 70.0. These results establish handoff readiness as a distinct, measurable dimension of GUI-agent assistance and demonstrate the value of explicitly managing it during execution.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

More than 1.3 billion people, approximately one in six worldwide, experience significant disability \citep{whodisability}, including visual, auditory, motor or tactile, and cognitive limitations. GUI agents can reduce barriers to everyday digital activities by operating interfaces on users' behalf. Yet completing an action and returning a usable result are different. A deaf user may be unable to follow up on a correctly scheduled appointment if captions are disabled. Someone relying on memory aids may be unable to check a completed shopping cart if the associated checklist is stale. A blind user may be unable to inspect retrieved facts left only in a visual representation.

We argue that accessible delegated assistance must meet both the ordinary digital goal and the \emph{handoff} conditions needed when control returns to the user. These conditions may concern accessible information, a durable support artifact, or a usable continuation interface. They are user- and task-conditioned. The same everyday activity can require different handoff states without changing its underlying goal. Previous matched-task and naturalistic studies likewise show that people with and without disabilities pursue familiar digital goals, while access requirements shape how results can be checked and continued \citep{a11ycua,arewethereyet}.

We introduce \bench, to our knowledge the first executable desktop GUI-agent benchmark that independently grades handoff requirements grounded in accessibility needs. It comprises 198 tasks across four functional groups and 14 user-need IDs derived from ISO/IEC 29138-1:2018 \citep{iso29138}. We use the international standard because it specifies functional user requirements independently of diagnostic labels, interface products, or agent implementations, providing a consistent basis for deriving observable completion conditions. We build on the WindowsAgentArena execution infrastructure \citep{waa}, with task-specific initialization and automatic checks of nominal task outcome ($T$), handoff readiness ($H$), and full success ($F=T\land H$). Three computer-science-trained annotators manually constructed the tasks over approximately 600 person-hours. Each task was executed and independently reviewed by the other two annotators across three rounds of cross-validation, including feasibility, task--need alignment, initial-state reproducibility, and evaluator correctness.

We evaluate representative GUI-agent baselines and identify recurring failures. Evidence is incompletely transferred across modalities or applications, saved support artifacts contain incorrect facts, accessibility settings are not reliably preserved, and agents terminate without verifying all delivery conditions. The outcome decomposition exposes both successful nominal execution with failed handoff and prepared support with incorrect nominal work. These results motivate \emph{LocalLSTC2}, a condition-driven method for maintaining a stable task contract throughout execution. It tracks immutable delivery conditions, verifies each condition before advancing, re-injects completed conditions as preservation constraints, retains cross-condition facts, and selects APIs relevant to the active condition. For controlled evaluation, we instantiate the method on LocalLSTC \citep{locallstc}, the strongest evaluated baseline. On a 60-task split, mean full success improves from 56.7 to 63.3, nominal task completion from 60.0 to 65.0, and handoff readiness from 63.3 to 70.0.

Our contributions are fourfold. First, we introduce the first executable accessibility-conditioned handoff benchmark, with 198 need-grounded GUI tasks and independently graded $T/H/F$ outcomes. Second, we provide a manually cross-validated construction and evaluation protocol grounded in an international user-need standard. Third, our comparative failure analysis identifies where strong GUI agents break the task--handoff contract. Finally, LocalLSTC2 raises task completion, handoff readiness, and full success through condition-driven execution.

\section{Related Work}

General computer-use benchmarks evaluate web, desktop, and mobile interaction through executable or trajectory-based protocols \citep{webarena,visualwebarena,osworld,waa,androidworld}. Recent datasets extend this coverage to professional and multimodal workflows \citep{spider2v,videogui,videowebarena,omnigui}. Their success criteria primarily measure whether the requested digital action was completed. \bench instead makes the accessibility-conditioned state returned to the user an independently graded part of success.

Accessibility-oriented studies establish why this distinction matters. A11y-CUA compares sighted and blind or low-vision participants on matched Windows tasks and shows that familiar goals can impose different interaction and monitoring requirements \citep{a11ycua}. A naturalistic study of blind screen-reader users documents ordinary editing and configuration tasks together with demands for explanation, recovery, verification, and user-controlled completion \citep{arewethereyet}. We operationalize one measurable prerequisite at the boundary between agent execution and user continuation, namely whether the terminal information, artifact, or interface state meets the documented need.

LocalLSTC retains active subgoals, evidence, and runtime feedback across bounded execution windows \citep{locallstc}. We use it as the base controller for LocalLSTC2 because its persistent state and explicit termination interface expose the task-contract boundary. The added method can wrap other controllers that permit context injection, action observation, and termination gating.

\subsection{Task and Handoff Contract}

An accessibility-conditioned delegated task is
\begin{equation}
\tau=(I,s_0,G,H_u),
\end{equation}
where $I$ is the instruction, $s_0$ is the initial desktop state, $G$ is the ordinary digital goal, and $H_u$ is the \emph{handoff contract} induced by documented need $u$. The contract maps the need to observable properties of the environment and user-facing outputs at termination. These properties fall into three recurring forms. Accessible information preserves task-relevant facts in an appropriate representation. Persistent external support records accurate information in a note, reminder, checklist, or bookmark. A usable continuation interface leaves an accessibility or input feature correctly configured.

Let $T^\tau(s_T)$ test the nominal goal and $H^\tau(s_T)$ test the handoff contract. Full success is
\begin{equation}
F^\tau(s_T)=T^\tau(s_T)\land H^\tau(s_T).
\label{eq:handoff-full}
\end{equation}
This definition separates the state required by the user from the channel chosen by the agent. A task may require Live Caption or Narrator to remain available after execution without requiring the agent to operate through that tool. For tasks that configure support directly, $G$ and $H_u$ may coincide. Such tasks contribute to overall $T$, $H$, and $F$, while independent-component analyses exclude them.

\subsection{Need-Grounded Construction and Evaluation}

The benchmark contains 198 tasks across visual, hearing, motor, and cognitive functional groups. Fourteen need IDs are derived from ISO/IEC 29138-1:2018, which specifies functional requirements independently of diagnoses and products \citep{iso29138}. We pair each need with one of eight everyday workflows spanning communication, information access, management, mobility, consumption, services, health, and accessibility configuration. CAPTCHA forms a separate verification stress test. The full taxonomy, workflow definitions, and distributions appear in Appendices~\ref{app:taxonomy}--\ref{app:statistics}.

Task construction begins with a documented need and a user scenario. Annotators identify the information, support artifact, or continuation capability required at handoff, formulate an observable $H_u$, pair it with $G$, and implement task setup and separate evaluators. A counterfactual check requires that removing the need change the handoff requirement. An alignment check requires the support to help the user inspect or continue the specific workflow. Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark-pipeline} illustrates this process with an audio-derived calendar event whose handoff contract preserves Live Caption. Nine complete construction and repair examples appear in Appendix~\ref{app:construction_examples}.

Each task resets a preconfigured Windows environment and runs task-specific setup on the WindowsAgentArena stack \citep{waa}. Evaluators inspect structured application state, saved artifacts, and required content when control returns. The released metadata retains the names \taskout, \accessreq, and \fullsuccess, which correspond to $T$, $H$, and $F$. Three computer-science-trained annotators constructed the tasks over approximately 600 person-hours. Every task was executed and independently reviewed by the other two annotators across three rounds covering feasibility, instruction clarity, need alignment, reproducible setup, and evaluator correctness. Implementation and validation details appear in Appendices~\ref{app:implementation} and~\ref{app:evaluators}.

The 198 tasks include 57 easy, 109 medium, and 32 hard instances. They exercise eight applications, with 110 tasks crossing application boundaries and 23 assigned to multiple need IDs. Median successful human operating time is 90.6 seconds. Figure~\ref{fig:coverage} shows how the functional groups are distributed across everyday workflows. Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark-statistics} reports the complementary distributions by functional group, workflow, subgroup, application involvement, application count, difficulty, and operating time.

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{HandoffGap}=P(T=1,H=0)

\section{Handoff-Aware Execution and Verification}
\label{sec:handoff-method}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/method.pdf}
\caption{LocalLSTC2 converts the instruction into immutable delivery conditions. It executes one condition at a time, retains cross-condition facts and completed-condition constraints, exposes APIs relevant to the active condition, and verifies every boundary before advancing.}
\label{fig:locallstc2-framework}
\end{figure*}

LocalLSTC2 preserves the complete task contract while focusing each decision on the condition currently being completed. At task start, the agent extracts a short ordered list $C=(c_1,\ldots,c_m)$ of immutable final verification goals. Requirements concerning one artifact or application state remain within one condition. Operational steps and intermediate tool calls are excluded, allowing recovery plans to change without rewriting the terminal contract.

The base agent addresses the conditions sequentially. The active condition is presented as the current goal, while verified conditions are re-injected as preservation constraints. A persistent fact map carries exact dates, codes, paths, quantities, identifiers, and requested wording across condition boundaries. Local execution context is reset after each verified condition so that obsolete subgoals and recovery traces do not distract later decisions.

LocalLSTC2 also filters software APIs through a lightweight relevance decision. Candidate methods are scored against the active condition, completed-condition constraints, persistent facts, execution history, and recovery feedback. Only selected descriptions enter the planner prompt, while GUI interaction remains available. This reduces prompt load and avoids irrelevant software actions without restricting the user's required terminal state. Full routing and prompt interfaces appear in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

When the planner proposes to finish a condition, a separate verifier receives the condition, its execution evidence, and the latest screenshot. Invisible persistent state requires a recent exact readback. Failure returns a targeted discrepancy for bounded repair. Success advances to the next condition and preserves the completed state. The final condition passes through the same gate before task termination. Persistent transcription and document-inspection channels provide reliable evidence for transient speech and layout-dependent documents, while the task instruction continues to determine the user-facing handoff state.

\subsection{Failure Mechanisms}
\label{sec:handoff-failures}

The strict terminal audit records 464 failures or partial failures with $F<1$, of which 361 have $H=0$. Inspected trajectories reveal four recurring breaks in the task-contract chain. Transient speech, visual content, or document layout is incompletely preserved before later actions. Cross-application transfer produces fluent but incorrect notes, reminders, or structured records. Accessibility settings and continuation interfaces are misconfigured or overwritten. Agents also terminate after a local change without rechecking the complete goal and handoff state.

These failures reflect familiar grounding, state-tracking, and long-horizon control problems, but the decomposed contract reveals their user-facing consequence. Fifty tasks fail under all four configurations, and 33 have $H=0$ for every method. Common cases include missed facts in transient captions, spatial relationships lost by linear PDF extraction, stale support artifacts, and exact settings left in the wrong state. The audit criteria, task counts, and representative trajectories appear in Appendices~\ref{app:failure} and~\ref{app:locallstc_case}.

Evidence transfer is the dominant mechanism for audio-derived tasks. For H1, failures occur in 28 of 44 Agent S3 runs, 39 of 44 CoAct runs, 23 of 44 LocalLSTC runs, and 30 of 44 HISA runs. Intermittent screenshots can miss a date, quantity, code, or negation in transient captions, while a later fluent artifact conceals the omission. Linear extraction from maps, images, and complex PDFs can similarly erase spatial relations before the agent records an answer.

Continuation-state failures are concentrated in exact configuration and precision interaction. For M2 tasks involving timing and keyboard control, failure rates range from 62.5\% to 93.8\% across methods. Agents also self-report completion in 233 failed runs across the four configurations, and 200 of these have $H=0$. These trajectories motivate a verifier that checks the complete delivery condition instead of accepting an attempted command or local visual change.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{General computer-use benchmarks.}
WebArena and VisualWebArena evaluate realistic web interaction \citep{webarena,visualwebarena}; OSWorld and WindowsAgentArena extend executable evaluation to desktop applications and operating-system settings \citep{osworld,waa}; AndroidWorld provides mobile environments \citep{androidworld}. Spider2-V, VideoGUI, VideoWebArena, and OmniGUI broaden the scope to professional data and multimodal workflows \citep{spider2v,videogui,videowebarena,omnigui}. These benchmarks establish reproducible environments, automatic setup, and state-based or trajectory-based evaluation. However, their primary success criterion is whether the requested digital action was completed. Even when successful runs leave an artifact or final screen for the user, they do not systematically vary and independently evaluate \emph{accessibility-conditioned handoff contracts}. \bench holds on to executable evaluation while making those contracts explicit.

\paragraph{Accessibility-oriented GUI agent studies.}
A11y-CUA compares sighted and blind/low-vision participants performing the same 60 Windows tasks and contrasts their interactions with agent behavior under default and accessibility-related conditions \citep{a11ycua}. It shows that familiar goals can involve different interaction and monitoring requirements, and that accessibility-conditioned input and display constraints can challenge current agents. The distinction in our work is between \emph{how agents act} and \emph{what they must leave for the user}: a user's reliance on an assistive modality need not become a mandatory agent execution channel.

Kodandaram et al. document 1,258 commands by eight blind screen-reader users in a three-week study involving a desktop GUI agent \citep{arewethereyet}. Alongside ordinary editing, file management, and application configuration, participants sought explanations, situational troubleshooting, recovery, learning support, and user-controlled completion. The observed grounding, planning, constraint-tracking, state-maintenance, and termination errors largely overlap with general GUI-agent weaknesses. Their consequences, however, are amplified when the user cannot readily inspect or repair the resulting state. This evidence motivates our focus on the \emph{boundary between automated work and user continuation}. Our benchmark measures a concrete prerequisite of that boundary: whether the terminal information, artifact, or interface state meets a documented need.



LocalLSTC organizes GUI-agent control into persistent long-term state and bounded short-term execution \citep{locallstc}. Its long-term controller retains active subgoals, evidence, and runtime feedback, while its short-term executor commits actions and feeds their outcomes back for planning, recovery, and termination. These mechanisms make it a strong baseline and a convenient reference implementation for LocalLSTC2. The condition-driven method itself operates at the task-contract boundary: it supplies the active delivery condition and retained constraints to a base GUI agent, observes the resulting action history, and intercepts proposed termination for verification. This interface can wrap alternative planning and action controllers without redefining their internal control organization.

\subsection{Task Definition: Handoff as a Necessary Condition}

Following executable computer-use benchmarks \citep{osworld,waa}, we represent interaction with the desktop as a partially observable Markov decision process
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{O},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{S}$ is the desktop state space, $\mathcal{O}$ the observation interface provided by an agent scaffold, $\mathcal{A}$ the allowed actions, and $\mathcal{P}$ the state transition dynamics. An agent receives observations, executes actions, and ends at state $s_T$ upon declaring completion or exhausting its action budget.

We define an accessibility-conditioned delegated task as
\begin{equation}
\tau=(I,s_0,G,H_u),
\end{equation}
where $I$ is the user instruction, $s_0$ the initial desktop state, $G$ the ordinary digital goal, and $H_u$ the \emph{handoff contract} derived from documented access need $u$. The access need describes a user requirement; the contract translates that need into observable properties of the environment and user-facing outputs that must hold when the agent returns control. It may require access to task-relevant information in an appropriate representation, a complete and persistent support artifact, or a usable accessibility/continuation state. Different needs can induce different $H_u$ for the same nominal goal $G$, and the same need may be realized by different contracts in different tasks. Thus, a need ID does not prescribe a particular assistive technology or agent execution strategy.

Let $T^\tau(s_T)=E_{\mathrm{task}}^\tau(s_T)$ test whether the nominal goal is met, and let $H^\tau(s_T)=E_{\mathrm{access}}^\tau(s_T)$ test the task's observable handoff contract. For binary tasks, full benchmark success is
\begin{equation}
F^\tau(s_T)=T^\tau(s_T)\land H^\tau(s_T).
\label{eq:handoff-full}
\end{equation}
The use of $H$ for the existing \accessreq score emphasizes its role: it tests \emph{handoff readiness}, not compliance with an agent-side tool prescription. For a need requiring handoff condition $H_u$, usable continuation logically requires that condition; satisfying it alone does not establish actual user comprehension, trust, or successful continuation. The benchmark therefore assesses an \emph{operational proxy for a necessary condition}, not a comprehensive measure of accessibility.

The user's need for assistive technology determines the required terminal state, without prescribing the agent's own perception or control channel. Independent task and handoff scoring is defined in Section~\ref{sec:benchmark-evaluation}.

\subsection{Handoff Conditions across Accessibility Needs}
\label{sec:handoff-contracts}
We distinguish three recurring user-facing handoff conditions: (i) \emph{accessible information}, in which task-relevant facts or media content remain available in a representation appropriate to the user's need; (ii) \emph{persistent external support}, such as an accurate saved reminder, checklist, note, or bookmark; and (iii) a \emph{usable continuation interface}, such as an enabled and correctly configured screen reader, captioning mode, or alternative input. These condition types can overlap and are not exclusive to any functional group. Their purpose is to specify the state the agent leaves for the user, not the tools it must use during execution.

For tasks with separately observable components, the counterfactual without the access need can retain the nominal goal $G$ while changing the handoff contract $H_u$. For tasks that directly configure assistive support, the goal and handoff condition may coincide. Both task types are retained, while independent-component analyses distinguish them.

\subsection{Task Construction}

The central design principle of \bench is \emph{evidence-informed, need-driven task construction}. The same nominal GUI task can involve different accessibility requirements depending on the user's access need. We therefore start with a documented need ID, determine the information, interaction capability, or support it requires in a specific user scenario, and express that requirement as an observable handoff condition $H_u$. We then pair it with an everyday digital goal $G$, instantiate the GUI workflow, and create executable checks for both components. This order makes the need central to task design and prevents an unrelated accessibility feature from being added to an otherwise generic task. A worked example at the end of this section traces the complete construction process.

\paragraph{Evidence-grounded need selection and task realization.}
ISO/IEC 29138-1 describes functional user-accessibility needs across interface technologies. We use it as the primary taxonomy because it separates what a user needs from diagnoses, individual assistive products, and an agent's implementation \citep{iso29138}. We selected 14 IDs relevant to GUI interaction and checked their operational meaning against user studies, accessibility surveys, standards, and platform guidance \citep{w3cpeople,w3cstories,webaimsr,w3cauditory,w3cphysical}. Our visual, hearing, motor, and cognitive groups index functional barriers rather than clinical categories; nine potentially overlapping ICF-informed subgroups provide finer analysis \citep{whoicf}. This makes needs comparable across applications while allowing distinct user scenarios.

\paragraph{Workflow coverage and construction.}
The need ID determines \emph{why} a task requires accessible support; the everyday workflow determines \emph{what} the user wants done. We use the WHO ICF Activities and Participation domains as a coverage guide and consolidate digital activities into the eight workflow types in Table~\ref{tab:method-workflows}, rather than inventing disability-exclusive activities. Crossing needs with workflows avoids confounding one user group with one application or task type. CAPTCHA is retained separately as a deterministic verification-barrier stress test because it is a prominent barrier for screen-reader users \citep{webaimsr}.

For each task, annotators (1) identify the relevant ISO-grounded need and user scenario, (2) specify the information, support artifact, or continuation capability required at handoff, (3) formulate an observable handoff condition $H_u$, (4) pair it with the digital goal $G$, and (5) construct the Windows workflow, initialization, and separate evaluators. A counterfactual check asks whether removing the need materially changes the handoff requirement; a second alignment check ensures that the support is relevant to verifying or continuing the specific workflow. These checks prevent arbitrary accessibility toggles from being attached to otherwise generic tasks. \todo{Complete and document the per-task alignment audit.}

\subsection{Environment and Execution}

To ensure a comparable handoff contract can be evaluated from the same initial state $s_0$, we reset a preconfigured Windows environment and execute task-specific setup functions before the agent begins. Depending on the task, setup can place source files or media on the desktop, open an application or webpage, restore a cart or local database, prepare an email or calendar state, or set an accessibility feature to a known value. We build on the OSWorld/WindowsAgentArena execution stack \citep{osworld,waa} and use common Windows applications such as Edge/Chrome, Thunderbird Mail and Calendar, Sticky Notes, Notepad, LibreOffice, Windows Settings, and built-in accessibility features.

Some web tasks use self-hosted or pinned services when a live website would make the initial state or expected answer unstable. These include shopping, CMS, Wikipedia, forum, and CAPTCHA workflows. Hearing tasks use short pinned media or fixed task-specific audio when reproducibility requires it. The benchmark therefore preserves normal GUI interaction while keeping the state seen by different agents comparable across runs. Deployment details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\label{sec:benchmark-evaluation}

Task-specific evaluators inspect the desktop when the agent returns control. They check (i) the nominal workflow result and (ii) the observable part of the task's need-conditioned handoff contract. The underlying evaluators comprise \textbf{structured-state checks} for settings, carts, calendar entries, mail fields, and spreadsheet cells; \textbf{artifact checks} for notes, files, checklists, and bookmarks; and \textbf{content checks} for task-relevant facts recovered from text, images, or audio. Where appropriate, natural-language output is checked for required facts, while dates, quantities, URLs, application state, and other exact values are checked structurally.

We retain the existing score names for compatibility with task metadata but clarify their interpretation: \taskout is $T=E_{\mathrm{task}}$; \accessreq is $H=E_{\mathrm{access}}$, the task-specific \emph{handoff-readiness check}; and \fullsuccess is $F=T\land H$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:handoff-full}). \textbf{Assistive-tool usage by the agent is not a metric}: enabling and preserving a tool for the user may be graded as $H$, but acting through that tool is not required unless a task explicitly evaluates the process, which this benchmark does not.

For the subset $D$ of tasks where $T$ and $H$ are independently observable, we report
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{HandoffGap}_D=P(T=1,H=0\mid D),
\label{eq:handoff-gap}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{HandoffGivenTask}_D=P(H=1\mid T=1,D).
\label{eq:handoff-conditional}
\end{equation}
The first is the joint frequency of an ordinary-task success with an unmet handoff condition (corresponding to the previous \argap); the second is the fraction of nominally completed tasks that are handoff-ready (the previous \condsat). These denominators must not be conflated: in general, $\mathrm{HandoffGap}_D \neq 1-\mathrm{HandoffGivenTask}_D$. We also distinguish $T=0,H=1$, in which user-facing support is correctly established but the underlying task remains incomplete. Coincident tasks---such as directly enabling Mouse Keys or creating a requested reminder---are included in overall $T/H/F$ but excluded from independent-component gap statistics. We separately test the validity of natural-language evaluators using paraphrases, omitted facts, near misses, negations, and format variants; Appendix~\ref{app:evaluators} specifies the validity study and its reporting denominators.

The evaluators thus operationalize a \emph{necessary terminal condition}: a setting flag, saved artifact, or structured content check is evidence of a handoff-ready state under the benchmark contract, not proof that a specific user can actually perceive, understand, or continue successfully. Mid-task communication, quality of explanations, and recovery under shared control remain outside the current measured construct.

\subsection{Manual Curation and Cross-Validation}
We build on the WindowsAgentArena task-execution and evaluation infrastructure \citep{waa}, extending task setup and executable checks for accessibility-conditioned handoff. Three annotators trained in computer science manually constructed the 198 tasks. Each task was independently reviewed and executed by the other two annotators over three cross-validation rounds. They checked feasibility, instruction clarity, need--task alignment, reproducible setup, and evaluator correctness, including whether valid states were accepted and plausible near-misses rejected. Issues were revised and rechecked before inclusion. Construction and validation required approximately 600 person-hours: about two hours of construction and one hour of review and revision per task. This procedure extends execution-based task curation with independent validation of the accessibility-specific completion condition.

\section{Handoff-Aware Execution and Verification}
\label{sec:handoff-method}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/method.pdf}
\caption{Condition-driven execution instantiated on task \texttt{management-service\_appointment\_audio}. The instruction is converted into fixed delivery conditions that are exposed to a base GUI agent one at a time. Condition-aware selection supplies only relevant software-level tools---illustrated by a Chrome API for the caption condition and \texttt{AudioTools.transcribe} for speech-to-text evidence---while GUI interaction remains available. A separate verifier gates each condition boundary; failures return targeted repair feedback, whereas passes advance with fresh local context while retaining completed-condition constraints and persistent facts. Screenshots show the corresponding Chrome and Thunderbird states.}
\label{fig:locallstc2-framework}
\end{figure*}

Preliminary trajectory inspection suggests that need-conditioned handoff requirements can be omitted or left unverified even when nominal work is completed, while a prepared support state can coexist with an incorrect task result. LocalLSTC2 addresses these failures with one organizing principle: \emph{preserve the complete set of user-facing delivery conditions while focusing each decision on the condition currently being completed}. This is an execution strategy for requirements already specified in the task, not a separate optimization objective. It does not require an explicit $T/H$ type label. Instead, it derives all requested final deliverables and states from the instruction, including ordinary task results and need-conditioned handoff requirements, and connects the active condition to execution context, retained facts, tool selection, and completion checks. The required base-agent interface is minimal: the method must be able to inject task context, observe action outcomes and history, and gate a proposed termination. Planning, grounding, action generation, and recovery remain properties of the wrapped GUI agent.

\paragraph{Fixed completion conditions.}
At task start, the agent generates a short ordered list $C=(c_1,\ldots,c_m)$ of immutable final verification goals. Requirements concerning one artifact or application state, including its contents, exact values, format, and saved status, remain in one condition rather than being split into action-level steps. Intermediate discoveries and tool calls are excluded. The resulting condition list is the stable task contract; operational subgoals may change without rewriting it.

\paragraph{Condition-wise execution with preservation constraints.}
The agent addresses $C$ sequentially. The active condition $c_i$ is exposed as the current verification goal, while the strings of previously verified conditions are re-injected as constraints that later actions must preserve. After each action, step abstraction summarizes the outcome and is instructed to report whether the action changed any completed condition. When a condition finishes, the next condition begins with fresh local execution context: local subgoals, summaries, recovery feedback, and episodic-memory state are reset, while the fixed condition list, completed-condition constraints, and persistent global facts remain. The fact map carries exact task-relevant information such as dates, codes, paths, identifiers, quantities, and requested wording across this reset.

\paragraph{Condition-aware API selection with a lightweight decision model.}
Software-level APIs associated with the applications in a task can be included together in a planner prompt even though many methods are irrelevant to the next decision. As the inventory grows, this increases prompt length and the number of distractor actions. LocalLSTC2 casts API filtering as a typed relevance decision: a lightweight decision model jointly scores candidate methods for the next action under the active condition, completed-condition constraints, persistent facts, textual execution history, and recovery feedback. Only selected method descriptions enter the planner prompt, while GUI and execution-side automation remain available. Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} specifies the decision model, inclusion rule, retained evidence tools, and failure behavior.

\paragraph{Condition-level verification and state transfer.}
Whenever the planner proposes to finish the active condition, a separate verifier call receives that condition, the current condition's execution history, and the latest screenshot. For an invisible persistent state, the verifier requires a recent exact readback in the supplied execution evidence rather than treating an attempted command or transient notification as completion. A failure leaves the condition active and returns the discrepancy for bounded repair. A pass records the condition as completed, advances to $c_{i+1}$, and exposes the completed-condition set as preservation constraints. The last condition is verified through the same interface before task termination. Thus, verification is attached to each delivery boundary, while preservation of earlier conditions is maintained through re-injected constraints and per-step change monitoring.

\paragraph{Evidence channels.}
Condition-driven execution is complemented by evidence channels for inputs that are unreliable under intermittent screenshots. Audio and video speech can be transcribed into a persistent text artifact; PDFs can be searched through full-document text extraction and inspected through rendered pages when layout matters. Relevant facts or artifact paths can then enter the persistent fact map for reuse by later conditions. These channels support evidence acquisition without replacing any user-facing accessibility or continuation state required by the instruction.

\section{Experiments and Analysis}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
We evaluate representative GUI-agent baselines on the 198 tasks of \bench, using matched Qwen3.8-27B planning backbones and maximum interaction budgets of 15 or 50 steps for the reported SR-15/SR-50 comparisons. We use the implementations and controlled-comparison protocol of LocalLSTC \citep{locallstc} as a starting point while retaining each baseline's native planning, grounding, action, and termination mechanisms. For the controlled method evaluation, we attach LocalLSTC2's condition-contract interface to the LocalLSTC baseline; this choice fixes the underlying controller while testing the added method. Adaptations to our Windows accessibility environment and newly introduced mechanisms are documented separately. HISA is evaluated without pattern memory. Appendix~\ref{app:baseline} distinguishes the original paper's shared inference parameters from settings that still require verification against our actual runs.

We score the mean task result $T$, handoff readiness $H$, and full success $F$ on a 0--100 scale; these are arithmetic mean evaluator scores, not necessarily binary run-success rates. On distinct-component tasks we additionally report $P(T=1,H=0)$ and $P(H=1\mid T=1)$; a strict trajectory audit classifies a run as failed or partially failed when its terminal full-success score is below one. We report scores by functional group, need ID, and workflow, with efficiency measurements and full configuration details in Appendix~\ref{app:baseline}. Our baseline experiments are conducted on \bench; OSWorld and WindowsAgentArena results cited for contextual comparison are not new runs in this study.

\subsection{Handoff-Centered Failure Analysis}
\label{sec:handoff-failures}

Our failure analysis asks \emph{where the transfer from agent execution to user-usable state breaks}. We distinguish the generic action-level mechanism (e.g., imprecise grounding, loss of constraints, weak state tracking, brittle cross-application navigation, or premature termination) from the \emph{handoff obligation} it violates. This avoids presenting familiar GUI failures as if they were mechanisms unique to particular disabilities. We organize the result records and inspected trajectories by the following handoff stages: (i) acquisition and preservation of task evidence; (ii) transfer of evidence across applications or modalities; (iii) accurate creation or updating of persistent user-facing artifacts; (iv) establishment and preservation of required assistive/continuation state; and (v) final verification and honest termination. Appendix~\ref{app:failure} gives the coding rule and distinguishes this audit synthesis from a formal multi-annotator study.

Across the current 198-task runs of Agent S3, CoAct, LocalLSTC, and HISA without pattern memory, all using Qwen3.8-27B, 464 of 792 terminal \texttt{result.txt} records are failures or partial failures under the strict criterion $F<1$. Of these runs, 361 (77.8\%) have $H=0$. The decomposition is important: 25 runs have $T=1,H=0$, so an ordinary task-success measure would conceal a failed accessibility-conditioned handoff, whereas 81 have $T=0,H=1$, meaning that support is present but the underlying work is wrong. The remaining 336 have $T=H=0$. This audit follows the failure definition \texttt{eval(result.txt) < 1}; Table~\ref{tab:main-accessneed} instead reports arithmetic mean scores at the fixed 50-step snapshot. For CoAct, the terminal and 50-step summaries differ, so their values must not be interchanged. The pooled audit counts are descriptive because the configurations share tasks, a model family, and parts of the environment.

\paragraph{Evidence does not survive to handoff.}
An agent may observe task-relevant speech or visual content yet fail to preserve the full evidence needed in a saved artifact or downstream application. H1, which requires audio information in another modality, fails in 28/44 Agent S3 runs, 39/44 CoAct runs, 23/44 LocalLSTC runs, and 30/44 HISA runs. A concrete observation-channel limitation appears in LocalLSTC: speech is sampled through intermittent screenshots of transient captions rather than a continuous transcript. A date, code, quantity, or negation can appear between observations or be overwritten, while a later fluent artifact hides the omission. Maps, images, and complex PDFs exhibit a related representation problem: linear OCR can destroy spatial relations, after which the agent sometimes presents a plausible inference as verified fact. The accessibility consequence is an incomplete or unreliable alternative representation.

\paragraph{The handoff artifact is plausible but wrong.}
The agent may create a reminder, checklist, note, or spreadsheet update while making an incorrect nominal decision in the underlying workflow. Conversely, it may finish the workflow while leaving the support artifact stale. Four hearing tasks produce $T=0,H=1$ under all four methods: \nolinkurl{health-long_covid_care_note}, \nolinkurl{information-ftc_washington_q4_fraud}, \nolinkurl{management-home_delivery_window_audio}, and \nolinkurl{service-cms_update_access_desk_page_audio}. The timer task \nolinkurl{management-quick_sponge_timer} produces the central $T=1,H=0$ handoff gap under all four. Neither direction is captured by an undifferentiated ``task completed'' message. Task facts and the downstream artifact or continuation state must therefore be tracked and checked separately.

\paragraph{The user-facing interface is not ready.}
A standard GUI grounding or settings-navigation error may leave a requested caption mode, on-screen keyboard, Mouse Keys configuration, magnification value, or screen reader inactive or incorrectly configured. Exact settings and delegated precision interaction are especially unstable. For M2, which includes timing and keyboard-control requirements, failure rates are 68.8\%, 81.2\%, 62.5\%, and 93.8\% for Agent S3, CoAct, LocalLSTC, and HISA; for M4 sustained or precise pointer interaction, they are 57.1\%, 71.4\%, 71.4\%, and 57.1\%. The underlying grounding and actuation errors are generic, but the accessibility consequence is direct: the requested continuation state is unusable, or the interaction barrier that motivated delegation remains. This does not imply that the agent must use the assistive feature itself; it requires independent verification of the state left for the user.

\paragraph{Premature termination breaks the handoff contract.}
Agents often stop after observing a local change, before rechecking the complete task, saved artifact, assistive state, or final view. Agent S3, CoAct, LocalLSTC, and HISA respectively self-report completion in 56, 52, 52, and 73 failed runs; 47, 47, 43, and 63 of those runs also have $H=0$. Long trajectories form a second general cluster: agents repeat ineffective clicks or scrolling, lose constraints across applications, exhaust the action budget, or leave too few steps for saving and verification. A pre-handoff audit should verify $G$ and $H_u$ separately and trigger bounded repair or an explicit incomplete-state report instead of an unqualified completion claim.

The overlap across methods indicates stable difficulty rather than one scaffold-specific defect. Fifty of 198 tasks fail under all four configurations, including 17 hearing, 16 visual, 9 motor, and 8 cognitive tasks; 33 tasks have $H=0$ for all four. Consumption accounts for 14 of the 50 common failures, followed by information (9), and communication, health, and mobility (5 each). Other recurring generic causes are imprecise grounding, incomplete conjunctive constraint tracking, cross-application transfer and save errors, and unsupported inference under uncertain perception. Appendix~\ref{app:failure} specifies the audit coding criteria; finer-grained need and workflow slices are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:needanalysis}.

\section{Conclusion}

We introduced \bench, an executable 198-task Windows benchmark that makes \emph{handoff readiness} an explicit necessary condition for delegated GUI assistance under documented accessibility needs. The framework separates the ordinary goal from the user-facing information, durable support, and continuation interface that must remain usable after the agent stops. This distinction explains why an assistive tool may be required \emph{for the user at handoff} without being required \emph{as an agent execution channel}, and why different accessibility needs can change task success without changing the underlying everyday activity.

Our decomposed evaluation distinguishes task completion $T$, need-conditioned handoff readiness $H$, and full success $F=T\land H$. In particular, $T=1,H=0$ isolates the gap that ordinary task-success metrics can overlook, while the more frequent $T=0,H=1$ outcome reveals apparently prepared but factually incorrect support. The fact that marginal $H$ exceeds $T$ for all four completed baselines reinforces the value of separately evaluating these requirements rather than assuming one score characterizes both. Trajectory analysis records omitted, misconfigured, and subsequently invalidated handoff conditions alongside incorrect nominal work. Motivated by these observations, LocalLSTC2 exposes specified delivery conditions to sequential execution, persistent facts, condition-aware API selection, preservation constraints, and condition-level verification. On the controlled 60-task split, it improves nominal task completion, handoff readiness, and full success. Together, the benchmark and method make observable handoff prerequisites an explicit part of evaluating and supporting accessible GUI assistance.


\section{Limitations}

Our handoff construct is intentionally narrower than accessible user experience. A correct saved note, checked setting flag, or final application view can be a necessary prerequisite for usability without proving that a particular user can find, interpret, verify, trust, or continue from it. The benchmark does not directly score interactive clarification, explanations, shared control, user consent, mid-task recovery, or the effort required for verification after handoff. A setting may also be enabled but fail to function in a particular environment or with a particular combination of assistive tools. Terminal-state evaluators necessarily approximate these richer outcomes.

The four functional groups and 14 documented needs are analytical categories, not complete descriptions of any individual or diagnosis. Users may have overlapping and changing needs and distinct preferences, so the contract appropriate for one user is not universally appropriate for another. Tasks are controlled, evidence-informed synthetic realizations rather than participant-generated workflows. External user validation is still needed, particularly for whether each handoff contract is meaningful in context. Requiring an assistive feature without a plausible link to the subsequent workflow would compromise construct validity. We therefore specify an alignment audit and flag instruction or evaluator mismatches for correction. In particular, examples whose recorded wording says that the agent must \emph{use} a specific assistive feature need to be reconciled with source-agnostic, end-state scoring. More broadly, a need-conditioned toggle that serves no plausible subsequent inspection or continuation purpose is not a valid handoff contract merely because it can be checked automatically.

The environment is limited to Windows and a fixed application mix, with self-hosted services and pinned media for reproducibility. Handoff content and tool states can be task-dependent, and deterministic evaluators may miss valid alternatives or user-facing defects outside their checks. The cross-method analysis covers four Qwen3.8-27B configurations on the same 198 tasks (792 configuration--task observations), while the current LocalLSTC2 evaluation uses a 60-task split. These repeated observations are not independent samples, and HISA is evaluated without pattern memory. Broader model and benchmark generalization, repeated-run uncertainty estimates, and individual ablations of fixed conditions, global facts, preservation constraints, and verification remain future work.


\section{Ethical Considerations}

A handoff contract should reflect a user's stated access needs and preferences, not prescribe universal behavior for a disability category. Leaving a feature enabled is appropriate only if the user requests or requires it in the particular setting. Unwanted changes to accessibility configurations can themselves create access barriers. Likewise, source-agnostic agent execution does not justify hiding uncertainty or overriding user choice. Assistive technology supports user agency and continuation, rather than obliging the user to hand over tasks to an autonomous agent. The benchmark is an evaluation tool, not evidence that assistive tools or participatory design can be replaced by automation.

Especially in health, financial, identity, and other consequential workflows, agent actions may require informed confirmation, transparent records, and a safe opportunity to interrupt or correct the process. Our end-state checks do not replace those safeguards. Group-level results should not be interpreted as estimates of individual capabilities or preferences, and future studies should involve users with relevant lived experience in validating handoff contracts and evaluation procedures.

\section{Implementation and Reproducibility Details}
\label{app:implementation}

\paragraph{Base-agent interface and reference implementation.}
Condition-driven execution wraps an iterative GUI agent through three integration points: context injection before the next decision, access to action outcomes and local history, and a termination hook that can defer completion and return repair feedback. It does not prescribe how the wrapped agent plans, grounds, generates actions, manages its own memory, or recovers from execution errors. In our reference implementation, these hooks are attached to LocalLSTC's training-free long/short-term controller \citep{locallstc}. Its existing action loop, state abstraction, context refinement, and recovery remain the base-agent implementation; the condition layer controls which delivery condition is active, what state crosses condition boundaries, and whether a proposed termination may advance. The method-specific interfaces are isolated in Section~\ref{app:locallstc2-prompts}.

\paragraph{Condition generation.}
Before interaction, the global planner receives the task instruction under a condition-generation prompt and returns one non-empty JSON list. Exact duplicate strings are removed, and the implementation accepts at most 12 high-level conditions. Each condition must describe one independently required final deliverable or application state, keeping its existence, contents, exact values, formatting, and saved status together. Intermediate discoveries, tool calls, and verification actions are excluded. Invalid output is repaired and retried up to the configured parse-retry limit; failure to obtain a valid list ends the run rather than falling back to unconstrained execution.

\paragraph{Condition state and context boundaries.}
Runtime state contains the immutable condition tuple, the active index, a list of completed condition strings, and a global fact map. A condition transition resets local subgoals, summaries, recovery feedback, stall state, and episodic-memory selection, and sets the visible action-history boundary to the start of the new condition. It retains the full condition tuple, completed-condition constraints, global facts, and the underlying action log. The planner receives the original task, completed conditions, the active condition, retained facts, current-condition history, recovery feedback, and the latest screenshot.

\paragraph{Persistent facts and preservation monitoring.}
Step abstraction may append a \texttt{GLOBAL:} block containing stable \texttt{key = value} facts. The parser rejects empty entries and keys that denote volatile UI state such as focus, selection, cursor position, or temporary visibility; keys and values are bounded to 128 and 1,024 characters, respectively. Facts with the same key are updated and the complete map is re-injected at every later planner step. If conditions have already been completed, the step-abstraction prompt additionally asks the state manager to name any completed condition changed by the latest action. This monitor is semantic evidence supplied to subsequent planning; completed-condition preservation is prompt-mediated rather than a controller-side state rollback.

\paragraph{Condition verification and repair.}
A planner \texttt{TERMINATE} action triggers a separate state-manager call before the active condition may advance. The verifier receives the original task, the active condition, the current local subgoal, action history since the current condition began, and the latest screenshot. It must return exactly \texttt{PASS: reason} or \texttt{FAIL: discrepancy}. For persistent state not visible in the screenshot, it requires recent exact-readback evidence in that history; the verifier does not itself query the environment. A failure retains the current condition and injects the discrepancy as recovery feedback. Verification failures count as stalls, and three consecutive stalls terminate the run. A pass appends the condition string to the completed list and starts the next condition with fresh local context. After the last condition passes this same verifier, the run terminates; the controller does not perform an additional aggregate re-verification of earlier conditions. The runtime verifier is separate from the benchmark evaluator and receives no evaluator ground truth.

\paragraph{Condition-aware API routing and episodic memory.}
Candidate API domains come from task \texttt{related\_apps}; instruction keywords additionally expose audio or Google Drive APIs, and PDF APIs are always available. Before each planner call, one batched JEV request to \texttt{Mapika/decider-2b} \citep{decider2b}, served through the configured decision endpoint, scores every candidate method against the textual planner context. Methods with $p(\texttt{true})\geq 0.10$ are exposed, with the threshold overridable at runtime; both PDF methods are retained regardless of score. Parsing retries for one planner step reuse the same selection. A JEV initialization or inference error ends the run instead of exposing the unfiltered inventory. A second, optional JEV decision chooses the latest safe subgoal anchor from which episodic history should be retained. This memory filter is evaluated as an efficiency component rather than a necessary part of condition-driven execution.

\paragraph{Audio, video, and long-document evidence channels.}
Audio and local video files are sent to a Parakeet ASR service; video audio is first converted by FFmpeg to 16-kHz mono WAV. For a web video, the implementation discovers an open Chrome page through the debugging endpoint, downloads the first media item with \texttt{yt-dlp}, and follows the same transcription path. Short transcripts are returned directly, whereas longer transcripts are saved to a fixed \texttt{audio.txt} path in the guest. PDF text extraction uses the document text layer and inserts explicit page markers; it does not perform OCR. Long extraction results are saved to \texttt{pdf.txt}. A second PDF API renders a selected page at 144 DPI by default and returns the image directly to the next planner call. Relevant facts or artifact paths may then be retained through the global-fact interface.

\subsection{Method-Specific Prompt Interfaces}
\label{app:locallstc2-prompts}

The following extracts contain only the interfaces introduced for condition-driven execution. Generic base-agent planning, action syntax, grounding, memory, and recovery instructions are omitted.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Condition extraction.}
\begin{quote}\small
Derive the immutable final verification goals from the instruction and return exactly one JSON list. Include only independently required final deliverables or final states. Keep all requirements of one artifact or application state in one goal; exclude intermediate discoveries, tool calls, and verification steps. Order goals by a reliable operational sequence.
\end{quote}

\item \textbf{Active-condition execution and preservation.}
\begin{quote}\small
Work on the single current verification goal while keeping every completed verification goal satisfied. Treat completed goals as immutable state constraints. If an unavoidable interaction changes one, restore its satisfied state before terminating the current subtask. Output \texttt{TERMINATE} only when the current goal is satisfied and completed goals remain satisfied.
\end{quote}

\item \textbf{Cross-condition facts and change monitoring.}
\begin{quote}\small
After the step summary, explicitly name every previously completed condition changed by the latest step, or state that all remain unchanged. If the step discovered exact information useful to later goals, append \texttt{GLOBAL:} with one stable \texttt{key = value} fact per line. Preserve exact wording and values; exclude volatile UI state.
\end{quote}

\item \textbf{Condition verifier.}
\begin{quote}\small
Return exactly \texttt{PASS:} with a brief evidence-supported reason or \texttt{FAIL:} with the missing or contradictory evidence. Pass only when every listed target is supported by the current condition's history and latest screenshot. If persistent state is not visible, require a recent exact readback. An attempted command, transient notification, partial result, guessed value, or uncertainty is not completion.
\end{quote}

\item \textbf{API relevance decision.}
\begin{quote}\small
For each candidate method: ``Should this API method be included in the planner context for the next action?'' Score the explicitly enumerated choices \texttt{true} (include) and \texttt{false} (not useful for the next action). Expose methods whose \texttt{true} probability meets the threshold.
\end{quote}

\item \textbf{Evidence-channel constraint.}
\begin{quote}\small
Transcribe provided audio or video before other actions. Whenever transcription is used, also enable Chrome Live Caption and leave it enabled. Treat transcription as speech evidence, not as a substitute for a requested user-facing caption state. For PDF-derived answers, inspect the relevant rendered source page rather than relying on extracted linear text alone.
\end{quote}
\end{enumerate}


\paragraph{Task schema.}
Each task records \texttt{id}, \texttt{user\_group}, \texttt{subgroup\_ids}, \texttt{category}, \texttt{need\_ids}, \texttt{difficulty}, \texttt{operation\_time}, \texttt{instruction}, \texttt{source}, \texttt{gt\_steps}, \texttt{config}, \texttt{related\_apps}, \texttt{evaluator}, and \texttt{proxy}. The \texttt{operation\_time} field stores the successful human execution time in seconds.

\paragraph{Self-hosted services.}
The current Windows implementation uses OneStopShop (7770), an e-commerce CMS/admin service (7780), Kiwix Wikipedia (8888), a Reddit-style forum (9999), and a local CAPTCHA service (8765).

\paragraph{Cross-run service isolation.}
A service manifest maps each mutable shared service to the IDs of tasks that access it. OneStopShop and the CMS receive host-global exclusive lanes because task setup and execution reset or modify their shared Magento state. Wikipedia and the forum are read-only in the current task set and are omitted from the manifest. CAPTCHA challenges are deterministic functions of challenge type and seed, and their evaluators inspect the success URL in each task's isolated browser rather than shared server records, so CAPTCHA is also omitted and its tasks run concurrently.

Each experiment run maintains a dynamic queue shared by its workers. Before claiming a task, a worker attempts to acquire every required global lane without blocking. If acquisition fails, the worker leaves that task pending and scans subsequent tasks; a short per-run queue lock makes task claiming atomic without serializing execution. When no pending task is currently runnable, the run registers once in a host-global FIFO waiter queue for the blocked service. New acquisitions cannot bypass an existing waiter, and the run moves to the back of the queue after claiming a service task. Consequently, contending runs interleave service tasks instead of allowing the current holder to repeatedly reacquire the lane. Acquired lanes are held across environment setup, agent interaction, and terminal evaluation, and are released only after the task container exits. Thus both workers within one run and workers in independently launched runs are prevented from concurrently accessing the same mutable backend while queued runs make progress in arrival order.

Lane acquisition precedes VM-overlay preparation and task-container launch. Consequently, lane waiting is outside the agent-side timer, which begins only after environment reset, and does not contribute to the reported per-task execution time, token count, or step count. We record per-worker lane-wait duration separately in a scheduler log. This separation preserves agent-efficiency measurements while making additional wall-clock delay from shared-service isolation observable.

\paragraph{Hearing-media provenance.}
For fixed TTS tasks, release metadata should include the canonical script, generated audio asset, provider/model/voice/version, expected continuous caption phrases or atomic facts, duration, required caption feature, generation date, and file hash. The construction script is never exposed to the agent as task input.

\section{Full Baseline Configuration and Reproducibility}
\label{app:baseline}

\paragraph{Comparison protocol.}
We evaluate Agent S3, CoAct, LocalLSTC, and HISA on the same 198 tasks with a Qwen3.8-27B planning backbone and reported 15/50-step budgets; HISA does not use pattern memory. The original LocalLSTC study preserves the native prompts, grounders, tools, and control logic of the compared frameworks when standardizing a planner backbone \citep{locallstc}. We use that protocol as a starting point but replace its OSWorld/WindowsAgentArena workloads with our Windows accessibility benchmark; original benchmark performance is not transferred to our setting.

\paragraph{LocalLSTC2 inference settings.}
The recorded LocalLSTC2 run uses \texttt{qwen3.8-27b} for the global planner, visual grounder, and state manager, with a 50-step interaction budget and a thinking-token budget of 2,048. The launcher inherits temperature $0$, \texttt{top\_p}=0.95, \texttt{top\_k}=20, and sampling seed $42$ from the shared runner defaults. Its planner response format permits one to three candidate proposals and selects one executable plan. Thinking and preservation of thinking context are enabled; reasoning effort is left unset and therefore uses the model backend default. Parameters not passed by \texttt{scripts/run\_locallstc2.sh} retain their parser defaults.

\paragraph{Observation, control, and tools.}
LocalLSTC2 is configured for a $1280\!\times\!720$ control space and uses the native dimensions of each captured screenshot for grounding, together with a 0.5-second post-action wait, a 50-pixel region-of-interest margin, context refinement every 10 steps and forced refinement after 20 steps, and a 300-second execution-side automation timeout. Three consecutive stalls terminate the run. Strict loop detection compares normalized action and result fingerprints and routes repeated no-progress behavior through the same recovery and stall mechanism. GUI, execution-side automation, and selected software APIs remain available throughout the run; the Windows accessibility backend is UI Automation.

\paragraph{LocalLSTC2 prompt changes.}
Agent S3, CoAct, and HISA retain their own planner coordination, memory, and termination strategies. The evaluated LocalLSTC2 instance adds method-specific prompt interfaces to the selected base agent: immutable-condition extraction, active-condition execution, completed-condition preservation, persistent-fact extraction, condition verification, API relevance decisions, and evidence-channel constraints. Section~\ref{app:locallstc2-prompts} reports compact extracts of these additions; generic base-agent planning, action-format, grounding, memory, and recovery text is intentionally omitted.

\section{Failure-Audit Criteria and Coding Rules}
\label{app:failure}


We analyze the current top-level results for Agent S3, CoAct, LocalLSTC, and HISA without pattern memory, each using Qwen3.8-27B on the same 198 tasks. A run fails when its executable evaluator score in \texttt{result.txt} is below one. The analysis uses all 792 result records and their available execution logs; it does not treat four configurations on the same task as independent samples. The mechanism labels below are an audit synthesis rather than a completed multi-annotator study, so no inter-annotator agreement is claimed.

We use two levels of description. The first names a generic execution cause: missing evidence, grounding or actuation error, constraint loss, cross-application transfer error, persistence/save failure, inference under uncertain perception, repeated recovery or budget exhaustion, premature termination, environment infeasibility, or scaffold/controller failure. The second records the accessibility-conditioned consequence: incomplete accessible information, a missing/stale/incorrect persistent artifact, an unestablished assistive or continuation state, a delegated interaction barrier that remains, increased user verification/recovery burden, or no handoff failure when $H=1$ but $T=0$. This separation prevents an ordinary GUI error from being redescribed as disability-specific while retaining its effect on the state returned to the user.

\subsection{Observation-channel limitation for transient captions}

The paired communication, consumption, and management cases in Figures~\ref{fig:case-communication}, \ref{fig:case-consumption}, and~\ref{fig:case-management} expose a limitation of the observation interface as well as of the planner.  The LocalLSTC baseline does not receive a continuous caption transcript or an audio stream.  It obtains screenshots at action and observation boundaries and must read whatever portion of the Live Caption overlay happens to be visible in those frames.  Captions are transient: text may appear and disappear between two screenshots, a later utterance may overwrite an earlier one, and a single frame may contain only a suffix of the dates, codes, quantities, alternatives, or ordering needed downstream.  Enabling Live Caption can therefore satisfy $H$ while leaving $T$ incomplete, as in the consumption and management cases ($T/H/F=0/1/0$).

The three figures separate related failure mechanisms on the same task records before and after the method change.  In the communication case, the baseline never acquires the recording evidence and produces neither a correct draft nor a valid terminal support state.  In the consumption case, Live Caption is ready but the requested product is absent from the cart.  In the management case, the support settings are ready but the four facts are not consolidated into the required Sticky Note.  LocalLSTC2 completes the same three tasks by retaining the delivery conditions across evidence acquisition, cross-application transfer, artifact construction, and final verification.  This comparison distinguishes observation coverage from later memory, transfer, and persistence failures without changing task identity between the baseline and repair evidence.

These failures are properties of the evaluated scaffold, not unavoidable properties of caption-based assistance.  A continuous caption-event stream, transcript buffer, denser temporally triggered observations, or an explicit replay-and-accumulate policy could expose otherwise missed evidence.  Preserving the caption feature and accurately completing the nominal task remain separate checks; the paired figures show why both must be verified before handoff.

\subsection{Complex-PDF case: layout-aware visual verification}
\label{app:polisher_pdf_case}

Task \nolinkurl{consumption-cheapest_polisher} provides a representative case in which a document's text layer is useful for retrieval but unreliable for final semantic verification. The source is a 57-page, untagged Harbor Freight catalogue with dense multi-column product blocks, interleaved names, item numbers, sale prices, comparison prices, and promotional text. Its embedded text is therefore not read in the same two-dimensional order in which the page is visually organized. The complete released instruction is:

\begin{quote}
\small
\textbf{Instruction.} I have difficulty using a physical keyboard. Open the Windows On-Screen Keyboard and leave it available for me. Find the cheapest polisher in the provided Harbor Freight catalogue and save its full product name exactly as shown on the first line and its price on the second line of \texttt{answer.txt} on the Desktop.
\end{quote}

A text-only strategy repeatedly formed plausible but incorrect name--price associations. On physical PDF page~6, extraction placed \texttt{\$19.99} before the line \texttt{6" PALM POLISHER} and \texttt{\$109.99} after its item number, making nearest-line matching assign the wrong price. Page~13 contained the lexically cheaper \texttt{6 PIECE POLISHER ACCESSORY KIT} at \texttt{\$14.99}, although it is an accessory rather than a polisher. On page~17, a nearby \texttt{\$9.99} belonged to a cut-off tool, while the actual \texttt{3" POLISHER KIT} cost \texttt{\$34.99}. These errors are not resolved by extracting more neighboring lines because the missing relation is spatial: product names and prices occupy aligned visual blocks rather than a reliable linear text sequence.

The successful trajectory used the two PDF APIs as complementary channels. First, \texttt{PdfTools.extract\_text} stored the complete text with explicit physical-page markers. A keyword search reduced 57 pages to candidate pages 6, 13, and 17. Second, \texttt{PdfTools.render\_page} rendered each candidate page and returned the PNG directly as the first visual input to the planner, avoiding viewer chrome, viewport cropping, and desktop-resolution downsampling. The agent first identified the \texttt{\$19.99} palm polisher on page~6. It then inspected page~17 to reject the text-derived \texttt{\$9.99} hypothesis, inspected page~13 to classify the \texttt{\$14.99} result as an accessory kit, and finally re-rendered page~6 at 200~DPI to verify the exact output string. It wrote \texttt{6" PALM POLISHER} and \texttt{\$19.99} to \texttt{answer.txt}, left the On-Screen Keyboard open, and received full executable credit. Table~\ref{tab:polisher-disambiguation} summarizes the disambiguation, and Figure~\ref{fig:polisher-pdf-evidence} shows the rendered evidence supplied to the model.

\section{Evaluator DSL and Validity Study}
\label{app:evaluators}

A compact evaluator notation expresses required text, alternatives, conjunctions, and constrained patterns:
\begin{verbatim}
"text"              = required expression
["a", "b"]          = any_of
{"all_of":[A,B]}   = require both
{"regex":"..."}   = constrained pattern
\end{verbatim}
For multi-fact outputs, score coverage over required atomic points and combine these checks with structured conditions such as recipient, date, file path, cart state, or accessibility setting. Evaluator validation includes canonical correct answers, paraphrastic variants, missing-fact cases, wrong-value near misses, negation, and formatting variation. Quantitative human agreement is outside the current validation protocol.

\section{Task Curation and Cross-Validation Details}

Three annotators trained in computer science manually constructed the benchmark. Each task authored by one annotator was reviewed and manually executed by the other two during three rounds of cross-validation. Review covered feasibility, instruction clarity, task--need fit, consistency with the documented need and its supporting real-user and accessibility evidence, reproducibility of the setup, and evaluator correctness. The counterfactual relevance check asked whether removing the access-specific requirement would leave the required information, artifact, persistent state, or evaluator essentially unchanged. Tasks failing this check were revised, reassigned, or removed. In total, construction and validation required approximately 600 person-hours, comprising about two hours of construction and one hour of validation and revision per task.

This cross-validation establishes internal construct and execution quality, but it does not substitute for participatory or large-scale validation with target users with disabilities. We therefore limit our claims to evidence-informed access-need relevance rather than exhaustive representation of all users, workflows, or preferences.
