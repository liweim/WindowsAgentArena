\title{\bench: Benchmarking GUI Agents for Users with Diverse Accessibility Needs}
\author{Anonymous ACL submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
GUI agents are typically evaluated by whether they complete a requested digital task, although task completion alone may not leave users with accessibility needs able to inspect or continue the result. Existing GUI-agent benchmarks do not systematically evaluate this accessibility-conditioned handoff as an independent requirement of success. We introduce \bench, an executable desktop benchmark comprising 198 tasks that jointly assess nominal task completion and handoff readiness. Evaluations of four representative baselines reveal failures that conventional task-outcome measures can overlook. Motivated by these findings, we develop LocalLSTC2, a condition-driven execution method that maintains and verifies task and handoff requirements throughout interaction. On a controlled 60-task split, LocalLSTC2 improves full success from 56.7\% to 63.3\% over its base agent. These results establish handoff readiness as a measurable dimension of GUI-agent performance and show the value of explicit requirement tracking for accessibility-conditioned assistance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

More than 1.3 billion people, approximately one in six worldwide, experience significant disability \citep{whodisability}. GUI agents could improve access to everyday digital services by carrying out interface operations on users' behalf. However, the state of an application after an agent finishes matters as much as the actions it has completed. For example, a correctly scheduled appointment may remain difficult for a deaf user to review when captions are unavailable. A completed shopping task may be difficult to verify for users with visual impairments when visual confirmation is inaccessible. These cases expose a gap between satisfying a digital instruction and returning a result that the user can inspect and continue.

We characterize this gap through \emph{handoff readiness}, the extent to which the information, artifacts, and interface state returned by an agent satisfy the accessibility requirements of the delegated task. Handoff readiness depends on the user's documented need and on the workflow being performed. The same digital goal can therefore have different valid terminal states for different users. Users with and without disabilities often pursue similar digital goals, although their requirements for interaction, verification, and task continuation may differ \citep{a11ycua,arewethereyet}. Yet prevailing executable GUI benchmarks primarily assess the requested digital outcome, without separately measuring whether its returned state meets such requirements. This leaves an important part of accessible assistance outside conventional success evaluation.

To address this evaluation gap, we introduce \bench, an executable Windows benchmark with 198 tasks spanning visual, hearing, motor, and cognitive functional needs. Its 14 need categories are grounded in ISO/IEC 29138-1:2018, which defines functional requirements independently of diagnostic labels and specific products \citep{iso29138}. Each task pairs an ordinary digital goal with an observable accessibility-conditioned handoff requirement. Building on WindowsAgentArena \citep{waa}, the evaluation independently measures task completion $T$, handoff readiness $H$, and their conjunction $F=T\land H$. The benchmark consequently distinguishes an agent that finishes the digital work without leaving a usable continuation state from one that prepares access support but fails to complete the underlying task. To support reproducible measurement, the tasks were manually constructed and cross-validated by three annotators, with independent checks of task feasibility, need alignment, initialization, and evaluator behavior.

Across representative GUI-agent baselines, the benchmark reveals failures that are obscured by a single task-success score. Information acquired in one modality is sometimes lost before it reaches the destination application, support artifacts can preserve incorrect facts, and accessibility settings may be left inconsistent with the requested handoff. These failures share a control problem. The agent needs to maintain both the digital goal and the required returned state as execution moves across applications and intermediate decisions. We therefore introduce LocalLSTC2, a condition-driven extension of LocalLSTC \citep{locallstc} that keeps terminal requirements stable while verifying progress condition by condition. A controlled comparison on 60 tasks improves full success from 56.7 to 63.3 and handoff readiness from 63.3 to 70.0. The improvement indicates that explicit delivery-condition tracking can strengthen execution without treating accessibility as a post hoc check.

Our contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
\item We define and operationalize accessibility-conditioned handoff readiness as an independently evaluated component of GUI-agent success.
\item We introduce \bench, a 198-task executable benchmark with need-grounded handoff contracts, reproducible environments, and independently validated evaluators.
\item We characterize recurrent task and handoff failures in representative GUI agents, and introduce LocalLSTC2 to improve requirement preservation and verification during execution.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

Executable GUI-agent benchmarks have expanded from web interaction to desktop and mobile environments \citep{webarena,visualwebarena,osworld,waa,androidworld}, while recent datasets extend evaluation to professional and multimodal workflows \citep{spider2v,videogui,videowebarena,omnigui}. These resources provide realistic instructions, interaction traces, and state-based success checks. Their evaluation protocols primarily establish whether the requested digital outcome was achieved. They do not systematically vary users' functional access requirements or independently grade the handoff state returned after task execution. \bench preserves executable assessment while extending the success criterion to this user-conditioned terminal state.

Research on accessible computer use identifies requirements that ordinary task metrics may fail to capture. A11y-CUA compares matched Windows tasks for sighted and blind or low-vision participants, showing that similar goals can involve different interaction and monitoring demands \citep{a11ycua}. Naturalistic observations of blind screen-reader users further document the importance of explanations, recovery, verification, and user control \citep{arewethereyet}. These studies motivate evaluating accessibility within the workflow rather than treating disability categories as task labels. Our benchmark complements their user-centered evidence by operationalizing a narrower and reproducible criterion for determining whether the terminal information, support artifact, or interface configuration satisfies an explicitly documented handoff requirement.

Runtime controllers provide a complementary basis for addressing the execution failures exposed by this evaluation. LocalLSTC maintains subgoal state, evidence, and runtime feedback across bounded interaction windows \citep{locallstc}. However, persistent execution context alone does not ensure that a need-conditioned terminal requirement remains satisfied as other work proceeds. LocalLSTC2 therefore adds explicit delivery conditions, preservation constraints, and verification gates around the existing controller. This design isolates the handoff requirement from the evolving action sequence without requiring a new planning backbone.

\section{Benchmark Design}
\label{sec:benchmark-design}

\subsection{Task and Handoff Contract}

An accessibility-conditioned delegated task is
\begin{equation}
\tau=(I,s_0,G,H_u),
\end{equation}
where $I$ is the instruction, $s_0$ is the initial desktop state, $G$ is the ordinary digital goal, and $H_u$ is the \emph{handoff contract} induced by documented need $u$. The contract maps the need to observable properties of the environment and user-facing outputs at termination. The resulting requirements concern the representation of task-relevant information, the accuracy of persistent support artifacts, or the configuration of the interface available for continued interaction. Each requirement specifies an observable terminal property that connects the delegated goal to the user’s documented need.

Let $T^\tau(s_T)$ test the nominal goal and $H^\tau(s_T)$ test the handoff contract. Full success is
\begin{equation}
F^\tau(s_T)=T^\tau(s_T)\land H^\tau(s_T).
\label{eq:handoff-full}
\end{equation}
This definition separates the state required by the user from the channel chosen by the agent. A task may require Live Caption or Narrator to remain available after execution without requiring the agent to operate through that tool. For tasks that configure support directly, $G$ and $H_u$ may coincide. Such tasks contribute to overall $T$, $H$, and $F$, while independent-component analyses exclude them.

\subsection{Need-Grounded Construction and Evaluation}

\bench contains 198 tasks covering visual, hearing, motor, and cognitive functional groups. We derive 14 need identifiers from ISO/IEC 29138-1:2018, using functional access requirements rather than diagnoses as the grounding unit \citep{iso29138}. Tasks connect these requirements to eight everyday workflow classes, including communication, information access, management, mobility, consumption, services, health, and accessibility configuration. CAPTCHA is included as a separate verification stress test. This separation between functional need and workflow allows the same general activity to be evaluated under different handoff requirements. Complete definitions and distributions are provided in Appendices~\ref{app:taxonomy}--\ref{app:statistics}.

Task construction proceeds from a documented functional need to a concrete workflow and an observable terminal contract. Annotators first identify what the user would need to inspect or continue the delegated result, then define the corresponding handoff condition separately from the ordinary digital goal. A counterfactual review checks whether removing the need changes that condition, while a workflow-alignment review checks whether the required support serves the specific task rather than functioning as an arbitrary accessibility toggle. Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark-pipeline} illustrates the process through task \texttt{management-service\_appointment\_audio}. The agent needs to create an exact 45-minute appointment from recorded speech and leave Chrome Live Caption enabled for subsequent access. Figure~\ref{fig:locallstc2-framework} uses the same task to illustrate execution control. Further construction and repair examples appear in Appendix~\ref{app:construction_examples}.

The benchmark executes against reproducible Windows states implemented on the WindowsAgentArena infrastructure \citep{waa}. Task-specific setup prepares application data and relevant source material, while separate evaluators inspect the nominal outcome and handoff condition after control returns. Checks use structured application state, persistent artifacts, and task-relevant content, with exact fields evaluated programmatically when possible. The resulting terminal-state measures assess whether necessary prerequisites for continuation are present. They do not establish whether an individual user can successfully complete the subsequent interaction. The released evaluator fields \taskout, \accessreq, and \fullsuccess correspond to $T$, $H$, and $F$. Three computer-science-trained annotators spent approximately 600 person-hours constructing the tasks. Each task was executed and reviewed independently by the other two annotators over three cross-validation rounds, covering feasibility, task--need alignment, initial-state consistency, and evaluator correctness.

The need taxonomy specifies why a particular support state is necessary, while the workflow taxonomy specifies the ordinary digital goal being delegated. This separation avoids equating disability categories with particular applications or activities. The counterfactual and alignment checks also rule out a superficial success criterion in which an unrelated accessibility toggle is added to a generic task. For example, keeping captions enabled is a meaningful handoff requirement when the user may need to review or continue an audio-mediated appointment workflow, not merely because the task mentions hearing access.

The 198 tasks include 57 easy, 109 medium, and 32 hard instances. They exercise eight applications, with 110 tasks crossing application boundaries and 23 assigned to multiple need IDs. Median successful human operating time is 90.6 seconds. Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark-overview} summarizes benchmark composition by workflow, functional group and subgroup, application involvement, application count, difficulty, and operating time.

\section{Handoff-Aware Execution and Verification}
\label{sec:handoff-method}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/method.pdf}
\caption{LocalLSTC2 execution on the same service-appointment task as Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark-pipeline}. The illustration shows the Live Caption condition already completed and the calendar-event condition active. The agent loop receives the completed-condition constraint, condition-relevant APIs, filtered history, and persistent event facts. A separate verifier either advances after a pass or returns the condition for repair.}
\label{fig:locallstc2-framework}
\end{figure*}

LocalLSTC2 addresses a specific weakness of long-horizon GUI execution. A planner can make local progress while losing track of terminal properties that must remain true when the user resumes control. The method represents these properties as an ordered set of immutable verification conditions $C=(c_1,\ldots,c_m)$, each describing a required final artifact or application state rather than an action sequence. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:locallstc2-framework}, the appointment example separates the preserved Live Caption setting from the calendar event still being completed. Keeping the verification conditions stable allows the agent to revise its action plan without changing what counts as success.

Execution advances through these conditions sequentially, with the active condition guiding the next bounded interaction. Once a condition is verified, its requirement remains in the planner context as a preservation constraint, reducing the chance that later actions undo an earlier result. A compact persistent fact map retains source-grounded dates, identifiers, quantities, paths, and exact strings needed across conditions. The map holds at most eight entries and admits facts supported by the instruction, a successful tool result, or observable application state. Before an update, the state manager consults existing entries so that revised evidence replaces the corresponding fact rather than creating conflicting duplicates. Condition-local interaction history can then be reset without discarding verified details needed by subsequent applications.

The separation between delivery conditions and action history matters during recovery. When a particular interaction fails, the controller may switch applications, retry through a different interface, or revise its local subgoal while retaining the same terminal criterion. Verified earlier conditions remain explicit even when their detailed traces are no longer in the active context. Persistent facts provide the evidence needed for cross-application transfer, while preservation constraints protect completed requirements from regression. Together, these mechanisms keep the agent's changing execution strategy aligned with the state eventually returned to the user.

To keep each decision focused, LocalLSTC2 selects software APIs by relevance to the active condition and the current execution evidence. The selector considers previously verified constraints, persistent facts, recent interactions, and recovery feedback before passing API descriptions to the planner. GUI interaction remains available, so selection reduces distracting tool context without narrowing the set of acceptable user-facing outcomes. Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} provides the routing interface and prompt details.

Condition completion is mediated by a separate verifier rather than inferred from the planner's intention to stop. The verifier inspects the target requirement, available execution evidence, and current screenshot. Where correctness depends on hidden persistent state, acceptance requires recent exact readback instead of an attempted command or transient notification. A failed check returns the observed discrepancy and keeps the condition active for bounded repair. A passed check advances execution while retaining earlier conditions as preservation constraints. The final condition undergoes the same procedure before termination. Additional channels for transient speech and layout-dependent documents help preserve evidence that ordinary screenshots or linear text extraction can miss.

\subsection{Failure Mechanisms}
\label{sec:handoff-failures}

The 50-step failure audit covers 442 failed or partially successful trajectories, including 369 in which the handoff requirement is unsatisfied. Inspection identifies recurring breakdowns at three points in execution. Information can be lost when transient speech, visual material, or document structure is converted into a persistent record. Subsequent application updates may then preserve fluent but incorrect content. Even when the underlying artifact is correct, agents can leave accessibility settings in the wrong state or terminate before checking whether all required outputs remain valid. These observations point to failures of evidence preservation and terminal-state control rather than to a single inaccessible interface component.

The decomposition makes such control failures visible in terms of their consequences for user continuation. For example, enabling Live Caption can satisfy a handoff requirement while intermittent screenshots still miss information needed to create the correct appointment. Conversely, the appointment may be accurate while captions remain unavailable for review. Across the four baseline configurations, 47 tasks fail consistently and 30 have unsatisfied handoff requirements in every configuration. Recurring cases involve transient captions, spatial relationships lost during document extraction, stale support artifacts, and incorrect final settings. The audit procedure and representative trajectories are documented in Appendices~\ref{app:failure} and~\ref{app:locallstc_case}.

Evidence transfer is particularly consequential for hearing-related tasks involving recorded speech. Within H1, 28 of 44 Agent S3 runs, 40 of 44 CoAct runs, and 27 of 44 runs for each of LocalLSTC and HISA fail. The underlying bottleneck often appears before the final application update. Intermittently sampled captions may omit a date, quantity, code, or negation, after which the agent creates a plausible but inaccurate persistent artifact. Spatial information extracted from maps or complex PDFs is vulnerable to an analogous loss of relationships during linearization.

Other failures arise when a workflow requires exact configuration or sustained precision. For M2 tasks involving timing and keyboard control, failure rates range from 62.5\% to 93.8\% across baselines. Trajectory inspection also finds agents that report completion after a local visual change without verifying the terminal application state. These cases motivate condition-level verification because an attempted action provides weaker evidence than a confirmed final requirement.

\section{Conclusion}

We introduced \bench to evaluate a dimension of GUI-agent performance that ordinary task-success metrics do not independently capture. Its 198 executable tasks measure both nominal digital outcomes and accessibility-conditioned handoff readiness. Evaluations expose failures in evidence transfer, persistent artifacts, continuation settings, and termination that can remain hidden in aggregate success scores. LocalLSTC2 addresses these failures through stable delivery conditions and verified progression, improving full success from 56.7 to 63.3 on a controlled 60-task split. Together, the benchmark and method provide a reproducible basis for studying whether agents return results that meet documented requirements for user continuation.

\section{Limitations}

Handoff readiness is an observable prerequisite for accessible continuation, not a direct measure of a user's experience. A correct artifact or enabled setting does not establish that a particular user can locate, interpret, trust, or continue from the result. The benchmark does not directly assess clarification, explanations, shared control, consent, or the effort of post-handoff verification. Assistive features may also behave differently across environments and configurations. Consequently, terminal-state evaluation should be understood as a bounded operationalization of accessibility rather than a substitute for user-centered assessment.

The four functional groups and 14 need categories describe task requirements rather than individuals or diagnoses. Access needs may overlap, change over time, and depend on personal preferences. Our tasks are controlled, evidence-informed constructions rather than participant-generated workflows, and their handoff contracts require validation with users who have relevant lived experience. In particular, a setting should count as a handoff requirement only when it plausibly supports inspection or continuation of the actual task. Instances that instruct an agent to use a specific assistive feature also require careful review against our source-agnostic terminal-state scoring, since an execution-channel instruction and a returned-state requirement are not equivalent.

The current benchmark is limited to Windows and a fixed application mix, using pinned media and self-hosted services to support reproducibility. Deterministic evaluators may reject valid alternative outcomes or miss defects outside their explicitly checked state. The baseline analysis uses four Qwen3.8-27B configurations over 198 tasks, yielding 792 configuration--task observations rather than independent task samples. LocalLSTC2 is evaluated on a controlled 60-task split, and HISA is run without pattern memory. Generalization to other backbones, operating systems, and user populations, along with repeated-run uncertainty estimation and finer-grained mechanism ablations, remains to be established.


\section{Ethical Considerations}

Accessibility-conditioned handoff requirements should reflect the user's stated needs and preferences rather than prescribe a uniform behavior for a disability category. Changing assistive settings without authorization can itself obstruct access, even when a benchmark would otherwise count the resulting state as valid. Agent autonomy should preserve opportunities for user choice, review, and correction. The benchmark evaluates specified terminal conditions and does not imply that automated agents can replace assistive technologies or participatory accessibility design.

Consequential workflows, including health, finance, and identity tasks, may require explicit confirmation, transparent records, and opportunities to interrupt or correct an agent. Terminal-state checks cannot replace these safeguards. Functional-group results should also not be interpreted as estimates of individual capabilities or preferences. Future validation should therefore involve people with relevant lived experience in defining and reviewing handoff contracts.

The functional categories motivate task construction, while the task contract specifies the observable state required for user takeover.

Each task has exactly one benchmark \texttt{user\_group} (visual, hearing, motor, or cognitive) and one or more \texttt{need\_ids}. The \texttt{need\_id\_to\_subgroup\_ids} crosswalk specifies \emph{eligible candidate subgroups}, not labels assigned automatically to every task with a given need. Annotators select only subgroup IDs warranted by the concrete scenario and observable user-facing accessibility requirement. Multiple IDs are allowed where more than one subgroup is actually addressed. For example, a C3 note/checklist task can warrant C-S2, whereas a C3 calendar/reminder task can warrant C-S3. Both are assigned only if both functions are explicitly required. Likewise M2 may support keyboard adaptation (M-S1), acceptance/timing adaptation (M-S3), or both. Group and subgroup annotations describe the accessibility condition to be supported at completion or user takeover, not the agent's required observation modality or interaction path. These labels are metadata for analysis, not clinical inference. Existing task records and subgroup-level statistics require task-by-task reassignment before they can be reported as finalized.

The everyday workflow categories are communication, information, management, mobility, consumption, service, health, and access. Motivated by prior evidence that CAPTCHA is a major accessibility barrier for screen-reader users \citep{webaimsr}, CAPTCHA is reported separately as a cross-cutting verification-barrier stress test rather than interpreted as a need or a daily-life workflow category. Each CAPTCHA task is assigned the underlying need exercised by its challenge modality or interaction demand.

\section{Implementation and Reproducibility Details}
\label{app:implementation}

\paragraph{Base-agent interface and reference implementation.}
Condition-driven execution adds three interfaces to an iterative GUI agent. The wrapper supplies task-level context before each decision, observes action outcomes and local history, and intercepts proposed termination to check the active delivery condition. Planning, visual grounding, action generation, and ordinary error recovery remain responsibilities of the underlying agent. Our reference implementation integrates these interfaces with the training-free long/short-term controller of LocalLSTC \citep{locallstc}. The added layer manages condition transitions, retained facts, and verification outcomes, without replacing the base agent's action loop or state abstraction. Section~\ref{app:locallstc2-prompts} specifies the method-specific prompts.

\paragraph{Condition generation.}
The global planner first converts the instruction into a nonempty JSON list of final-state conditions. Each condition describes one independently required artifact or application state and keeps its content, exact values, formatting, and persistence requirements together. Intermediate actions and evidence-gathering steps are excluded because they do not constitute deliverables. The parser removes duplicate strings and accepts at most 12 conditions. Invalid responses undergo bounded repair attempts, and a run ends if no valid list can be obtained. This prevents an unverified extraction failure from silently reverting execution to an unconstrained task.

\paragraph{Condition state and context boundaries.}
Execution maintains an immutable condition sequence, an active-condition index, completed-condition records, and a map of persistent facts. Advancing to the next condition clears local subgoals, summaries, recovery messages, stall counters, and episodic-memory selection. It also moves the visible history boundary to the start of the new condition while retaining the underlying action log. The original instruction, completed conditions, retained facts, active-condition history, recovery feedback, and latest screenshot remain available to the planner. These boundaries reduce interference from obsolete local context without discarding evidence needed to preserve earlier results.

\paragraph{Persistent facts and preservation monitoring.}
After each GUI, API, or execution-side action, step abstraction can emit a \texttt{GLOBAL:} block of source-grounded \texttt{key = value} facts. Only information supported by the instruction, successful tool output, or clearly observed application state is eligible. Tentative candidates, incomplete enumerations, guesses, and completion claims are excluded. The abstraction prompt receives the existing fact map, allowing the state manager to update the exact key when a logical fact changes and omit unchanged entries. The parser normalizes key syntax, rejects empty and volatile UI-state fields, bounds keys to 128 characters and values to 1,024 characters, and retains at most eight entries without evicting earlier facts. It also suppresses duplicate absolute paths and URLs. The complete map is supplied at later planning steps so that evidence acquired under one condition remains available under subsequent conditions. A separate preservation check asks the state manager whether the latest action altered any previously completed condition. This is prompt-mediated monitoring, not an environment-level rollback guarantee.

\paragraph{Condition verification and repair.}
When the planner proposes \texttt{TERMINATE}, the condition verifier evaluates the active condition before execution can advance. It receives the instruction, active condition, current subgoal, condition-local action history, and latest screenshot, and returns either \texttt{PASS: reason} or \texttt{FAIL: discrepancy}. Evidence for persistent state that is not visible in the screenshot is expected to appear as a recent exact readback in the supplied history. The verifier cannot query the environment independently and does not access benchmark evaluator ground truth. A failure preserves the active condition and converts the discrepancy into recovery feedback. Three consecutive stalls end the run. A pass records the condition as completed and resets local context for the next condition. Passing the final condition ends execution, without an additional controller-side audit of all earlier conditions.

\paragraph{Condition-aware API routing and episodic memory.}
The API inventory is selected from task-specific \texttt{related\_apps} and additional modalities implied by the instruction. Audio and Google Drive methods can be exposed through instruction keywords, while PDF methods remain candidates for every task. Before planning, a batched JEV query to \texttt{Mapika/decider-2b} \citep{decider2b} scores each candidate method against the current textual context. Methods meeting $p(\texttt{true})\geq 0.10$ are included, subject to a configurable threshold, and both PDF methods are retained regardless of score. Retries within the same planning step reuse the selection. A decision-service failure ends the run rather than exposing the entire inventory. An optional second decision selects a safe subgoal boundary for retaining episodic history. We evaluate this memory filter as an efficiency component, not as a defining requirement of condition-driven execution.

\paragraph{Audio, video, and long-document evidence channels.}
The evidence interfaces distinguish transcription from the accessibility state delivered to the user. Local audio is transcribed with a Parakeet ASR service, while local video is first converted to 16-kHz mono WAV with FFmpeg. For web video, the implementation identifies an open Chrome page through its debugging endpoint, retrieves the first media item using \texttt{yt-dlp}, and follows the same transcription path. Short transcripts return directly, and longer transcripts are stored in the guest as \texttt{audio.txt}. PDF text extraction preserves physical-page markers but does not perform OCR. Long text results are written to \texttt{pdf.txt}, while a separate API renders selected PDF pages at 144 DPI by default for visual inspection. Source facts and artifact locations can subsequently be retained through the persistent-fact interface.

\subsection{Method-Specific Prompt Interfaces}
\label{app:locallstc2-prompts}

The following extracts contain only the interfaces introduced for condition-driven execution. Generic base-agent planning, action syntax, grounding, memory, and recovery instructions are omitted.

\begin{systembox}[title={Condition Extraction Prompt}]
Derive the immutable final verification goals from the instruction and return exactly one JSON list. Include only independently required final deliverables or final states. Keep all requirements of one artifact or application state in one goal. Exclude intermediate discoveries, tool calls, and verification steps. Order goals by a reliable operational sequence.
\end{systembox}

\begin{systembox}[title={Active-Condition Execution and Preservation Prompt}]
Work on the single current verification goal while keeping every completed verification goal satisfied. Treat completed goals as immutable state constraints. If an unavoidable interaction changes one, restore its satisfied state before terminating the current subtask. Output \texttt{TERMINATE} only when the current goal is satisfied and completed goals remain satisfied.
\end{systembox}

\begin{systembox}[title={Cross-Condition Facts and Change Monitoring Prompt}]
After the step summary, explicitly name every previously completed condition changed by the latest step, or state that all remain unchanged. If the step directly observed or changed information that is confidently supported and useful to later goals, append \texttt{GLOBAL:} with one stable \texttt{key = value} fact per line. Preserve exact wording and values. Record at most eight facts, reuse an existing semantic key when its value changes, and exclude volatile UI state, guesses, tentative candidates, partially checked lists, unverified derived conclusions, and completion claims.

The prompt then lists every currently stored \texttt{key = value} fact. Compare the latest observation with all listed facts. If the same logical fact changed, emit the exact existing key with its new value. Add a new key only when no existing key expresses the same meaning. Do not repeat unchanged facts. They remain stored automatically.
\end{systembox}

\begin{systembox}[title={Condition Verifier Prompt}]
Return exactly \texttt{PASS:} with a brief evidence-supported reason or \texttt{FAIL:} with the missing or contradictory evidence. Pass only when every listed target is supported by the current condition's history and latest screenshot. If persistent state is not visible, require a recent exact readback. An attempted command, transient notification, partial result, guessed value, or uncertainty is not completion.
\end{systembox}

\begin{systembox}[title={API Relevance Decision Prompt}]
For each candidate method: ``Should this API method be included in the planner context for the next action?'' Score the explicitly enumerated choices \texttt{true} (include) and \texttt{false} (not useful for the next action). Expose methods whose \texttt{true} probability meets the threshold.
\end{systembox}

\begin{systembox}[title={Evidence-Channel Constraint Prompt}]
Transcribe provided audio or video before other actions. Whenever transcription is used, also enable Chrome Live Caption and leave it enabled. Treat transcription as speech evidence, not as a substitute for a requested user-facing caption state. For PDF-derived answers, inspect the relevant rendered source page rather than relying on extracted linear text alone.
\end{systembox}


\paragraph{Task schema.}
Each task records \texttt{id}, \texttt{user\_group}, \texttt{subgroup\_ids}, \texttt{category}, \texttt{need\_ids}, \texttt{difficulty}, \texttt{operation\_time}, \texttt{instruction}, \texttt{source}, \texttt{gt\_steps}, \texttt{config}, \texttt{related\_apps}, \texttt{evaluator}, and \texttt{proxy}. The \texttt{operation\_time} field stores the successful human execution time in seconds.

\paragraph{Self-hosted services.}
The current Windows implementation uses OneStopShop (7770), an e-commerce CMS/admin service (7780), Kiwix Wikipedia (8888), a Reddit-style forum (9999), and a local CAPTCHA service (8765).

\paragraph{Cross-run service isolation.}
A service manifest maps each mutable shared service to the IDs of tasks that access it. OneStopShop and the CMS receive host-global exclusive lanes because task setup and execution reset or modify their shared Magento state. Wikipedia and the forum are read-only in the current task set and are omitted from the manifest. CAPTCHA challenges are deterministic functions of challenge type and seed, and their evaluators inspect the success URL in each task's isolated browser rather than shared server records, so CAPTCHA is also omitted and its tasks run concurrently.

Each experiment run maintains a worker-shared task queue. A worker claims a task only after obtaining all required global service lanes through nonblocking acquisition. If a lane is unavailable, the task remains pending while the worker searches for another runnable task. A short run-local queue lock makes claims atomic without serializing agent execution. Runs blocked on a shared service enter a host-global FIFO queue, where existing waiters take precedence over new acquisitions. After receiving a service task, a run returns to the end of the queue. This policy prevents independent runs from repeatedly overtaking one another when accessing mutable backends. Lanes remain held through environment setup, agent execution, and terminal evaluation, and are released when the task container exits.

The scheduler acquires service lanes before preparing the VM overlay or launching the task container. Consequently, contention delay is excluded from the agent timer, which starts after environment reset, and does not affect reported interaction steps, token counts, or per-task execution time. Separate scheduler logs record worker-level lane-wait duration, making the wall-clock cost of service isolation observable without conflating it with agent efficiency.

\paragraph{Hearing-media provenance.}
For fixed TTS tasks, release metadata should include the canonical script, generated audio asset, provider/model/voice/version, expected continuous caption phrases or atomic facts, duration, required caption feature, generation date, and file hash. The construction script is never exposed to the agent as task input.

\section{Full Baseline Configuration and Reproducibility}
\label{app:baseline}

\paragraph{Comparison protocol.}
We evaluate Agent S3, CoAct, LocalLSTC, and HISA on the same 198 tasks with a Qwen3.8-27B planning backbone and reported 15/50-step budgets. HISA does not use pattern memory. The original LocalLSTC study preserves the native prompts, grounders, tools, and control logic of the compared frameworks when standardizing a planner backbone \citep{locallstc}. We use that protocol as a starting point but replace its OSWorld/WindowsAgentArena workloads with our Windows accessibility benchmark. Original benchmark performance is not transferred to our setting.

\paragraph{LocalLSTC2 inference settings.}
The recorded LocalLSTC2 run uses \texttt{qwen3.8-27b} for the global planner, visual grounder, and state manager, with a 50-step interaction budget and a thinking-token budget of 2,048. The launcher inherits temperature $0$, \texttt{top\_p}=0.95, \texttt{top\_k}=20, and sampling seed $42$ from the shared runner defaults. Its planner response format permits one to three candidate proposals and selects one executable plan. Thinking and preservation of thinking context are enabled. Reasoning effort is left unset and therefore uses the model backend default. Parameters not passed by \texttt{scripts/run\_locallstc2.sh} retain their parser defaults.

\paragraph{Observation, control, and tools.}
LocalLSTC2 is configured for a $1280\!\times\!720$ control space and uses the native dimensions of each captured screenshot for grounding, together with a 0.5-second post-action wait, a 50-pixel region-of-interest margin, context refinement every 10 steps and forced refinement after 20 steps, and a 300-second execution-side automation timeout. Three consecutive stalls terminate the run. Strict loop detection compares normalized action and result fingerprints and routes repeated no-progress behavior through the same recovery and stall mechanism. GUI, execution-side automation, and selected software APIs remain available throughout the run. The Windows accessibility backend is UI Automation.

\paragraph{LocalLSTC2 prompt changes.}
Agent S3, CoAct, and HISA retain their own planner coordination, memory, and termination strategies. The evaluated LocalLSTC2 instance adds prompt interfaces governing immutable-condition extraction, active-condition execution, preservation of completed conditions, persistent-fact extraction, condition verification, API relevance decisions, and evidence-channel constraints. Section~\ref{app:locallstc2-prompts} reports compact extracts of these additions. Generic base-agent planning, action-format, grounding, memory, and recovery text is intentionally omitted.

\section{Failure-Audit Criteria and Coding Rules}
\label{app:failure}


We analyze the current top-level results for Agent S3, CoAct, LocalLSTC, and HISA without pattern memory, each using Qwen3.8-27B on the same 198 tasks. A run fails when its executable evaluator score in \texttt{result.txt} is below one. The analysis uses all 792 result records and their available execution logs. It does not treat four configurations on the same task as independent samples. The mechanism labels below are an audit synthesis rather than a completed multi-annotator study, so no inter-annotator agreement is claimed.

The audit distinguishes the execution mechanism from its consequence for accessible handoff. Generic mechanisms include missing evidence, incorrect grounding or actuation, constraint loss, cross-application transfer errors, persistence failures, uncertain inference, repeated recovery, premature termination, environment infeasibility, and controller failures. The corresponding handoff analysis asks whether the final state lacks accessible information, retains an incorrect artifact, omits an assistive setting, leaves a delegated interaction unresolved, or transfers additional verification work to the user. A case with $H=1$ and $T=0$ is recorded as task failure without a handoff failure. Separating cause from consequence avoids treating an ordinary GUI error as inherently disability-specific while preserving its effect on the delivered state.

\section{LocalLSTC--Qwen3.8-27B Case Study}
\label{app:locallstc_case}

Two audited LocalLSTC cases illustrate how the form of available evidence constrains terminal verification. The baseline uses Qwen3.8-27B for planning and GTA1-7B for visual grounding. We examine transient captions and PDF layout because both can produce plausible intermediate observations without supplying the evidence required for a correct final state. Reported percentages refer to executable terminal-state evaluations unless explicitly noted otherwise.

\subsection{Observation-channel limitation for transient captions}

The communication, consumption, and management traces in Figures~\ref{fig:case-communication}, \ref{fig:case-consumption}, and~\ref{fig:case-management} reveal a temporal limitation of screenshot-based caption observation. LocalLSTC receives neither a continuous caption-event stream nor a direct audio channel. It observes only the portion of the Live Caption overlay visible when a screenshot is captured. Earlier words can disappear before the next observation, and a frame can preserve only a fragment of the dates, codes, quantities, or alternatives needed downstream. As a result, enabling Live Caption can satisfy handoff readiness while leaving the task outcome incomplete, as reflected by $T/H/F=0/1/0$ in the consumption and management cases.

The matched traces locate the resulting failures at different stages of the workflow. In communication, the baseline does not acquire the recording evidence, so neither the draft nor the required support state reaches a valid terminal condition. In consumption, the caption setting is available, but the requested product is absent from the cart. In management, the support settings persist, but the Sticky Note lacks the required four facts. The corresponding LocalLSTC2 runs retain the delivery conditions through evidence acquisition, transfer, artifact creation, and verification. These comparisons distinguish observation loss from failures of memory, cross-application transfer, and persistence without changing the evaluated tasks.

The observation limitation is specific to the evaluated interface, rather than intrinsic to caption-based assistance. Continuous caption events, a transcript buffer, temporally triggered observations, or replay with accumulation could recover information missed between screenshots. Even when additional evidence is available, the final caption state and the task artifact remain independently verifiable obligations. The paired cases therefore motivate checking both at handoff.

\subsection{Complex-PDF case: layout-aware visual verification}
\label{app:polisher_pdf_case}

Task \nolinkurl{consumption-cheapest_polisher} illustrates a mismatch between linear text extraction and the spatial evidence required for document interpretation. The source is a 57-page, untagged Harbor Freight catalogue whose dense product blocks interleave item names, identifiers, sale prices, comparison prices, and promotional text. Although the embedded text layer supports keyword retrieval, its reading order does not preserve the two-dimensional grouping of product names and prices. The released instruction specifies the required artifact and accessible input state as follows.

\begin{quote}
\small
\textbf{Instruction.} I have difficulty using a physical keyboard. Open the Windows On-Screen Keyboard and leave it available for me. Find the cheapest polisher in the provided Harbor Freight catalogue and save its full product name exactly as shown on the first line and its price on the second line of \texttt{answer.txt} on the Desktop.
\end{quote}

The extracted text yielded several plausible but incorrect name--price associations. On physical page~6, \texttt{\$19.99} precedes \texttt{6" PALM POLISHER}, while \texttt{\$109.99} follows its item number in the linearized output. Page~13 contains \texttt{6 PIECE POLISHER ACCESSORY KIT} at \texttt{\$14.99}, which is an accessory rather than the requested tool. On page~17, a nearby \texttt{\$9.99} refers to a cut-off tool, not the \texttt{3" POLISHER KIT} priced at \texttt{\$34.99}. Reading more neighboring lines does not reliably resolve these ambiguities because the relevant relations are defined by visual alignment within product blocks.

The successful run separated candidate retrieval from visual verification. \texttt{PdfTools.extract\_text} first produced text with physical-page markers, allowing keyword search to reduce the 57-page document to pages 6, 13, and 17. \texttt{PdfTools.render\_page} then supplied direct page images without browser chrome or viewport cropping. Page~6 established the \texttt{\$19.99} palm polisher candidate. Rendering pages 17 and 13 ruled out the cheaper-looking text matches by restoring their actual name--price and product-type relationships. A final rendering of page~6 at 200~DPI confirmed the exact output string. The agent wrote \texttt{6" PALM POLISHER} and \texttt{\$19.99} to \texttt{answer.txt}, kept the On-Screen Keyboard open, and received full executable credit. Table~\ref{tab:polisher-disambiguation} records the ambiguities, and Figure~\ref{fig:polisher-pdf-evidence} provides the visual evidence.

\section{Evaluator DSL and Validity Study}
\label{app:evaluators}

A compact evaluator notation expresses required text, alternatives, conjunctions, and constrained patterns:
\begin{verbatim}
"text"              = required expression
["a", "b"]          = any_of
{"all_of":[A,B]}   = require both
{"regex":"..."}   = constrained pattern
\end{verbatim}
For multi-fact outputs, score coverage over required atomic points and combine these checks with structured conditions such as recipient, date, file path, cart state, or accessibility setting. Evaluator validation includes canonical correct answers, paraphrastic variants, missing-fact cases, wrong-value near misses, negation, and formatting variation. Quantitative human agreement is outside the current validation protocol.

\section{Task Curation and Cross-Validation Details}

Three computer-science-trained annotators constructed the benchmark. Each task was authored by one annotator and manually reviewed and executed by the other two over three rounds of cross-validation. Review addressed feasibility, instruction clarity, documented need alignment, accessibility evidence, reproducible initialization, and evaluator correctness. A counterfactual relevance check examined whether removing the access-specific requirement would leave the information, artifact, persistent state, or evaluation contract essentially unchanged. Tasks that failed this check were revised, reassigned, or excluded. Construction and validation required approximately 600 person-hours, corresponding to about two hours of authoring and one hour of validation and revision per task.

\newpage
This review provides evidence of internal consistency across task instructions, initialization procedures, and terminal evaluators. It does not establish that the selected requirements fully represent the priorities or lived experiences of users with the corresponding access needs. Participatory validation is therefore needed to determine whether the evaluated terminal states support meaningful inspection and continuation in real use.
